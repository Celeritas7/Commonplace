Cardboard Workshop

Supermarket boxes make good temporary furniture. Corrugated board follows the same rules as sheet metal. It starts as a flat blank, you score it before you fold it, it is stronger in one direction, and a folded-over edge stiffens it the way a hem does. This page applies those rules to drawers, shelves and tables made from boxes you can get for free.

01 · Same rules, softer sheet

What carries over from sheet metal

Most of what you learn on the Sheet Metal sheets applies here. The numbers are looser, because board is soft and every box is a little different. Expect ±1–2 mm.

02 · Sourcing

Picking boxes at the supermarket

Best

Ask for these

  • Banana / fruit boxes. Usually double wall, often two-piece with a deep lid, and built to stack. They make the best carcasses.
  • Printer-paper / copier boxes. Strong, square, with a lid that fits. They make good drawers as they are.
  • Beverage and bottle cartons. They often come with ready-made egg-crate dividers.
  • Appliance boxes (from the electronics aisle). You get big flat sheets for tops and back panels.
Check

Before you carry it home

  • Press a corner. If it's soft or crushed, the box has lost most of its strength.
  • Look at the cut edge to count the plies and see the flutes.
  • Read the box print. A stamp like “5 ply”, “ECT 44”, or a bursting/BCT figure tells you the grade.
  • Take a tape measure and the shopping list from the planner below.
Avoid

Leave these behind

  • Meat, fish or dairy boxes, and anything damp, stained or smelly. These attract mould and cockroaches.
  • Waxed or plastic-coated produce boxes. Glue won't stick to them. They're fine for a dry bin, though.
  • Boxes that have been wet and dried. They look fine but are weak.
03 · Your stock

Design from the boxes you actually have

Don't design around a box. Design in flat panels, the way a sheet-metal part starts as a blank. Every build on this page comes down to a list of rectangles with score lines. Section 06 fits those rectangles onto the pieces you list here, so any mix of random boxes will do.

1 · Break down

Cut each box apart along its corner creases and flap creases. A flattened box gives you four wall panels and up to four flaps, all clean rectangles. Trim off crushed edges, torn tape and wet spots.

2 · Read the board

Measure the thickness at a cut edge. To find the flute direction, look at the edges. Where you can see the wavy pattern, the flutes run away from that edge, across the sheet. Check by bending it gently: the board bends easily along the flutes and resists across them.

3 · Mind old creases

An existing crease is a weak line. Use it as one of the part's own folds, or put it somewhere hidden. Never let one run across a shelf, a deck or a drawer bottom. If a piece has a crease you can't avoid, cut it there and enter it as two pieces.

4 · Too big? Splice

If a blank won't fit any piece, split it at a fold line and give one half a 25 mm glue flap, so the joint is hidden in a corner. On a flat panel, butt the two pieces and glue a 60 mm doubler strip over the seam on the inside, flutes crossed.

                      08 · Will it hold?

                      Load on top of the carcass

                      A box's stacking strength comes from its four walls acting as columns. The usual packaging estimate is the McKee formula: BCT ≈ 5.87 · ECT · √(t · Z), where Z is the box perimeter. Cutting a face open, turning the box on its side, humidity and long-term load each take a share of that strength away. The number below is a rough guide only.                       09 · Organiser

                      Egg-crate dividers for a drawer

                      Interlocking strips with half-depth slots, like the inserts in bottle cartons. Each strip braces the others, so they also stiffen the drawer. Slot width = board thickness + 0.5 mm, so the strips slide in without crushing.

                      10 · More builds

                      Other furniture from boxes

                      Shelving

                      Cube bookcase

                      1. Use same-size boxes, turned so the open face points forward.
                      2. Cut the opening but leave a 40 mm frame round it. The lip works like a flange and keeps the box square.
                      3. Zip-tie or glue neighbouring boxes together. A grid of boxes is much stiffer than single boxes.
                      4. Put heavy items (books) in the bottom row only. Check the load with section 08.
                      Table

                      Side table / nightstand

                      1. Use a cube-ish box standing upright, as shipped.
                      2. Fill it with an egg-crate grid at full height (section 09). That gives it internal columns.
                      3. Top: two layers glued with flutes crossed, overhanging 20 mm, with the edges hemmed or taped.
                      4. Light loads only: a lamp, a phone, books. Don't sit on it.
                      Desk

                      Laptop / monitor riser

                      1. Make triangular prism beams. Score a strip into 3 equal faces plus a 25 mm glue flap, fold and glue. Flutes run along the beam.
                      2. Set 3–4 beams on edge. They are very stiff for the board they use.
                      3. Glue on a cross-laminated top. Height = face width × 0.87.
                      Storage

                      Under-bed drawer

                      1. Make a shallow, wide tray using the planner with 1 drawer and the hem on.
                      2. Laminate a second base layer with flutes crossed. Wide bases sag.
                      3. Add a strap or rope loop through two slots ≥ 20 mm below the top edge to pull it out.
                      Entry

                      Shoe rack

                      1. Use a tall box on end with the front removed.
                      2. Fold Z-shaped shelves: a 15° slope with a 30 mm toe-stop lip at the front.
                      3. Glue each shelf to L-angle runners on the side walls. It's the same runner as in the drawer cut list.
                      4. Line the shelves with an old plastic bag, because shoes carry moisture.
                      Parts

                      Legs and columns

                      • Square tube: 4 faces + glue flap, flutes along the length. Stuff it with a rolled-up offcut so the walls can't buckle inward.
                      • Paper-roll cores (from shops or fabric stores) are the strongest free legs.
                      • Always cut leg ends square so the load bears on all walls.
                      11 · Kit

                      Tools and glue

                      Cutting
                      • Snap-blade box cutter. Snap to a fresh tip often, because a blunt blade crushes the flutes.
                      • Steel rule or straight edge. Cut against it in 2–3 light passes.
                      • Cut on scrap board, never on the floor or table.
                      Scoring
                      • Empty ballpoint pen, butter knife back, or a bone folder.
                      • Score along the rule, fold away from you over a table edge for a crisp 90°.
                      • Folds across the flutes need a firmer score than folds along them.
                      Joining
                      • PVA (white) wood glue for anything that carries load. Clamp with binder clips and leave it overnight.
                      • Hot glue is quick for positioning, but it creeps under load and softens in hot rooms.
                      • Gummed paper tape is better than plastic packing tape on edges. It bonds into the liner.
