Life calculation p.350–354

Rolling-bearing life is governed by fatigue of the raceways. The basic rating life L10 (reached by 90% of a group) follows from the basic dynamic load rating C and the equivalent dynamic load P:

L10 = (C / P)p  (millions of revolutions) p = 3 for ball bearings, p = 10/3 for roller bearings. C = basic dynamic load rating (kgf), from the bearing table.
Lh = (106 / 60n) · (C / P)p  (hours) n = speed (rpm). Equivalent form often used: fL = fn·(C/P), where fL is the fatigue-life factor and fn the speed factor read from charts.
Operating-condition factor

When sizing for an application, the load is multiplied by a working factor before computing life: continuously acting steady load ≈ 1.0–1.2; intermittent / variable ≈ 1.2–1.5; shock loads ≈ 1.5–3 (per the duty). Use the next page's factor fz values for the exact case.

Equivalent bearing load p.361–362

A combined radial Fr and axial Fa load is reduced to a single equivalent load using the bearing's X and Y factors:

Dynamic: P = X·Fr + Y·Fa  ·  Static: P0 = X0·Fr + Y0·Fa If P0 < Fr, take P0 = Fr. Deep-groove static: P0 = 0.6Fr + 0.5Fa. Angular-contact static: P0 = 0.5Fr + Y0Fa.

X and Y switch at the limit e, which itself depends on the axial-to-static-rating ratio Fa/C0:

Speed limits p.358–360

The limiting speed depends on bearing type, size, lubrication, cage design, clearance and accuracy. As a guide it varies inversely with the mean diameter:

nlim ≈ A / dm,   dm = (D + d)/2 A = a constant from Table 101 (larger for oil than grease). High-speed work needs raised accuracy (P6, P5), better lubrication and special cages.
Rough ranking (grease → oil raises the limit)
  • Highest: deep-groove ball, single-row angular-contact, self-aligning ball.
  • Medium: cylindrical roller, taper roller.
  • Lowest: thrust ball and thrust roller bearings.

Thrust bearings also need a minimum axial load to avoid skidding: Fa,min = k·(n/100000)² × (load-rating term), with k from the bearing type. Exact Table 101 constants are dense in the scan — take them from the catalogue / OCR.

Tolerances & shaft/housing fits p.402–418

The ring that rotates relative to the load needs an interference fit (it would otherwise creep); the ring that is stationary relative to the load can take a looser fit. So rotating-inner-ring applications use a tight shaft fit and a looser housing fit.

Typical fits (rotating inner ring, normal load)
  • Shaft: k5 / k6 / m6 (heavier load → tighter).
  • Housing (non-locating): H7 / G7 so the outer ring can float for thermal expansion.
  • Housing (locating, point load): K7 / M7 / N7.

Mounting arrangements p.419–437

A satisfactory mounting must satisfy five principles:

  1. The shaft must be axially located in both directions.
  2. The arrangement must accommodate relative axial (thermal) expansion between shaft and housing.
  3. Provide proper fits on shaft and housing for the load and rotation conditions.
  4. Provide lubricant circulation / retention.
  5. Match actual load conditions to the bearing capacity.

The classic solution is a locating + non-locating (“fixed/floating”) pair: one bearing takes axial load both ways; the other slides axially to absorb expansion.

Pre-loading

Pre-load removes internal clearance and reduces deflection under working load, exploiting the fact that deflection rate falls as load rises. Two bearings (or two rows) are adjusted against each other for a permanent axial load — applied to angular-contact, taper-roller and thrust ball bearings, and to NNK double-row cylindrical rollers on spindle noses via a 1:12 tapered bore. Excessive pre-load causes overload and overheating — set it carefully.

Sealing of rolling bearings p.438–440

Seals retain lubricant and keep out dirt and moisture. Two families:

  • Contact seals (lip / felt seals) — positive sealing for low-to-moderate speeds; rub on the shaft, so surface finish and hardness matter (see Rotary shaft seals, Notebook 1).
  • Non-contact seals (gap, labyrinth, flinger) — no friction or wear, suited to high speed; rely on a narrow gap or grease-filled labyrinth.

Many bearings are supplied pre-sealed (2RS) or shielded (ZZ). For grease-packed bearings the labyrinth or shield retains grease while excluding contaminants.

Hydrodynamic (journal) bearings p.441–452

A journal bearing carries load on a pressurised oil film generated by the rotation itself: the journal climbs the clearance, dragging oil into a converging wedge that develops pressure. There is no metal contact once the film is established, so wear is negligible.

Mean specific pressure  p = P / (l · d) P = radial load (kgf), l = journal length, d = journal diameter. Design also fixes the clearance ratio, minimum film thickness h0, eccentricity ratio X, oil viscosity η and the heat balance.

Design proceeds from the load, speed and viscosity to the minimum film thickness h0 (which must exceed the combined surface roughness of journal and bearing), the friction coefficient μ, the oil flow Q, and a heat balance between heat generated H and heat dissipated by the oil flow (H₁) and through the bearing metal (H₂). The handbook gives the governing equations and design charts across p.441–452.

Key checks
  • h0 > (R1 + R2) surface roughness sum — keeps the film unbroken.
  • Mean pressure p within the allowable for the bearing material.
  • Heat balance: generated heat ≤ dissipated heat at the chosen mean temperature.
