Gradient descent

In one line
Stand on the loss surface, feel which way is downhill (the gradient), take a small step that way, repeat until you reach the bottom.

The idea

A model has parameters — for a line, a weight $w$ and bias $b$. Each choice of $(w,b)$ gives some error on the data, measured by a loss. Plotting loss against the parameters makes a bowl-shaped surface. Gradient descent finds the lowest point by repeatedly stepping opposite the gradient, scaled by a learning rate $\eta$. Too small and it crawls; too large and it overshoots and diverges. Everything from linear regression to deep networks is trained this way.

$$\hat y = wx+b \qquad L=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat y_i-y_i)^2$$ $$w \leftarrow w-\eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}, \qquad b \leftarrow b-\eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}$$

Derivation, step by step

Step 1 — hypothesis

Predict with a straight line: $\hat y = wx + b$.

Step 2 — loss (MSE)

Penalise squared error so big misses hurt more, and the surface stays smooth: $L=\tfrac1n\sum(\hat y-y)^2$.

Step 3 — loss for one sample

For a single point, $L_i=(\hat y_i-y_i)^2$ with $\hat y_i = wx_i+b$.

Step 4 — chain rule

$\dfrac{\partial L_i}{\partial w}=\dfrac{\partial L_i}{\partial \hat y_i}\cdot \dfrac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial w}$. Part A: $\dfrac{\partial L_i}{\partial \hat y_i}=2(\hat y_i-y_i)$. Part B: $\dfrac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial w}=x_i$ (and $=1$ for $b$).

Step 5 — combine

$\dfrac{\partial L_i}{\partial w}=2(\hat y_i-y_i)x_i$, $\quad \dfrac{\partial L_i}{\partial b}=2(\hat y_i-y_i)$.

Step 6 — average over all samples

$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\dfrac2n\sum(\hat y_i-y_i)x_i, \quad \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b}=\dfrac2n\sum(\hat y_i-y_i)$. Plug into the update rule and loop.

Test yourself

Recall before flipping.

Why step opposite the gradient?
The gradient points uphill (steepest increase); we want to reduce loss, so we go the other way.
What does η (learning rate) control?
Step size. Too small → slow; too large → overshoot and diverge.
Gradient of MSE w.r.t. w?
$\frac{2}{n}\sum(\hat y-y)x$ — error, weighted by the input, averaged.
Batch vs stochastic GD?
Batch uses all samples per step; SGD uses one (or a mini-batch) — noisier but faster per update.
