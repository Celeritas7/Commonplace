The Mistake Bank — turning failures into drills

You already keep your failed attempts instead of deleting them. That instinct is right, and this lesson turns it into a system — because a mistake you have re-solved twice is one you stop making.

8 sections~11 minLinks to your Mistake Bank
1

Why this matters

Look at your own beginner practice notebook and you will find cells labelled failed, half successful with printing, Successful, Successful with efficiency.

You kept the failures. Most people delete them the moment something works, which throws away the most useful thing in the file — the record of what you actually got wrong.

A working solution teaches you little on re-reading; you understand it already. A failure you have to re-solve teaches you exactly the thing you did not know. This lesson is about doing that on purpose.

2

The mental model

The principle is that a mistake is only learned when you have re-solved it from scratch, later, without looking. Reading your old fix feels like learning and is not — recognition is not recall.

So the record has to contain the problem, not the solution. Four fields:

The trigger

The smallest code that reproduces it. Three lines, not thirty.

What you expected

One sentence — the step-1 description from 5.3.

What actually happened

The output or the error's last line, verbatim.

The category

Which kind of mistake it was. This is what makes patterns visible.

Note what is not in the record: the fix. Keep that separately, folded away, so re-solving is possible.

📖 From your notes

Your Golden_Problem_Template.ipynb already has this shape — Problem Statement → Attempt 1, Raw Thinking (DO NOT EDIT) → Attempt 2, Fixed by Me (Later) → Verified Working Solution. The “DO NOT EDIT” on attempt 1 is exactly the right instinct: preserving the wrong version is what makes the record useful.

After a dozen entries the distribution tells you something. If four of them are type confusion, the fix is not “be more careful” — it is the habit from 1.6: convert at the boundary, every time.

A worked entry:

bank-entry.md
# Entry 7 — 2026-09-04

## Trigger
rows = [{"qty": "5"}, {"qty": "12"}]
total = sum(r["qty"] for r in rows)

## Expected
17

## Actual
TypeError: unsupported operand type(s) for +: 'int' and 'str'

## Category
type confusion — values from a file are strings

<details> fix — int(r["qty"]) at the boundary </details>

Two weeks after banking a mistake, which review actually teaches you?

Re-solve it from scratch. Reading a fix you already understand produces a strong feeling of competence and almost no learning — you are recognising, not retrieving.

Retrieval is the thing that works. Opening the trigger cold, being unable to fix it, and having to think it through again is uncomfortable and is precisely why it sticks.

This is why the template hides the fix. It is also why option (c) is nearly worthless: a passing test tells you the code works, not that you could write it.

A practical cadence: review a week later, then a month, then a quarter. Anything you re-solve cleanly twice in a row can be retired from the bank.

6

What trips people here

🗑️
Banking typos

A misspelled variable is not a mistake worth keeping — you will not learn anything from re-solving it. Bank only mistakes that came from a wrong model of how something works. If you can say “I thought X, and actually Y”, it belongs.

📚
Writing the entry after you have fixed it

By then you have lost what you actually believed at the time, and the entry becomes a description of the fix. Write the trigger and the expectation while still stuck — that is when the wrong model is visible.

🏔️
Letting the bank grow without pruning

Fifty entries you never revisit is a graveyard, not a bank. Retire anything you have re-solved cleanly twice, and keep it small enough that a review is fifteen minutes.

8

Quick recap

What to carry forward

  • Keep the failure, not just the fix. The working version teaches you least.
  • An entry is four fields: trigger · expected · actual · category. The fix stays hidden.
  • Re-solve, don't re-read. Recognition feels like learning and isn't.
  • Categories reveal patterns — four type-confusion entries point at one habit, not four accidents.
  • Bank wrong models, not typos. “I thought X, actually Y” is the test.
  • Review at a week, a month, a quarter. Retire anything re-solved cleanly twice.
